A good reading page starts making promises before the first sentence is finished. The line ends where the eye expects it to, the next one is easy to find, and the margin gives the argument somewhere to breathe. These things are easy to miss while they are working.

I used to notice typography mostly when it called attention to itself. The type on the spine of a book, the number on a tram, the glass lettering over a shop door. Those things have a small job and sometimes do it with great flair. A long essay has a different problem. It asks someone to stay with an argument through all its turns, then offers the eye a few clues about where those turns are.

The clues can be quiet. An opening paragraph can sit a little larger than the rest. A heading can give the next idea a name. A quotation can occupy its own space long enough for a second voice to come through.

> The paragraph is a useful unit of doubt. It gives a thought enough room to be tried before it is asked to support the next one. A new paragraph can be an admission that the first thought needed company.

A reader rarely arrives at the top of a page with an empty afternoon. There may be a book open on the desk, a train nearly at its stop, or a question worth settling before dinner. The page has to work for someone who will leave it and return.

## The shape of a line

A long line makes a quiet demand. When the eye reaches the end, it has to find the beginning of the next one without mistaking it for a neighbour. A short line makes a different demand. It interrupts the sentence so often that the reader starts to hear the interruption. The [measure of a line](https://practicaltypography.com/line-length.html) gives a writer somewhere between those two problems to work.

On a phone there is less room to negotiate. The column can shrink, the type can change size, and the line breaks will move. The order of the ideas, the difference between a quotation and its surroundings, and the small pause before a new section still need to come through.

***

## Where the draft begins

There are three things I want from the page while I am writing:

1. **A place to begin.** The opening paragraph gets its own treatment. Its work is to give the reader a reason to read the second.
2. **A place to turn.** A heading belongs at a change in the argument. A reader who returns to the piece can find that turn again by following the [section link](#where-the-draft-begins).
3. **A place to leave a source.** The link can sit inside the sentence. A note can hold a detail that would otherwise take the sentence too far from its subject.[^note]

[^note]: A note has an address of its own and a link back to the sentence that called it.
